# Opening the Conversation After a Green Light

You've got the nonverbal ok — the returned look, the double take, the body angled your way (see [Offline Signals: The Body-Language Equivalent of Swiping](nonverbal-signals.md)). This note covers the next ninety seconds: crossing the gap, saying the first thing, and getting past the first exchange into an actual conversation.

The core fact: **once the cue is given, delivery matters far more than content.** The signal already did the persuading. Your opener's only job is to be easy to answer.

## The Window

- Act inside roughly thirty seconds. A green light is a state, not a standing invitation — it decays, and the same approach five minutes later arrives cold.
- Don't wait for a better moment. Waiting reads as hesitation, and the second look you're waiting for often doesn't come because *they* concluded you weren't interested.
- Don't over-prepare in the gap. Scripting during those thirty seconds is what makes the delivery stiff — see [Inner Blocks and Light Social Anxiety](blocks-social-anxiety.md).
- If the window closes, it isn't gone: a later, warmer "I meant to come say hi earlier" works fine and admits the truth.

## Crossing the Gap

- **Approach from the front or side**, never from behind or into their blind spot
- **Stop further away than feels natural** — about arm's length plus a bit. Closing distance is something you earn over the next minute.
- **Angle slightly rather than squaring up** — face-on at close range is confrontational; a slight angle is conversational and leaves them an exit
- **Speak at a volume they don't have to strain for**, once — repeating a failed opener kills the frame more than the noise does
- **Slow down.** Nerves speed everything up; deliberate pace is the single most reliable confidence signal you control.
- **Lead with the smile, not the sentence.** The face arrives before the words and sets how the words land.

## The First Thing You Say

Three openers work, and they're all short:

- **The honest one** — "I saw you looking over and figured I'd come say hi." Names the signal, skips the pretext, and is the strongest option when the cue was clear. Honesty *is* the technique here, same as in [Direct Contact Initiation and Assertiveness](direct-contact-initiation.md).
- **The situational one** — a remark about the shared context: the band, the queue, the dog, the weather doing something absurd. Low stakes, easy to answer, needs no cleverness.
- **The practical one** — a small real request or offer: a recommendation, a spare seat, help with something. Gives the interaction an initial reason to exist.

What matters more than which:

- **Make a statement, not an interrogation.** "This queue is a disaster" is easier to answer than "so how's your night going?" — the first invites agreement, the second demands a performance.
- **Keep it under two sentences.** Long openers signal you're negotiating against yourself.
- **Don't lead with a compliment on appearance.** It puts them in the position of evaluating you evaluating them. If you compliment, make it about something they chose — see [Flirting Techniques](flirting-techniques.md).
- **Never use a memorized line.** Delivery is the whole thing, and memorized text has a distinctive dead quality that people register instantly even when they can't name it.
- **Skip the apology.** "Sorry to bother you" frames yourself as an intrusion and invites them to agree.

## Getting Past the First Exchange

The opener isn't the hard part — the second beat is, and it's where most interactions die.

- **Give free information.** Volunteer a detail about yourself rather than asking another question. "I'm only here because my friend dragged me out" hands them three things to grab.
- **Trade statements, don't stack questions.** Three questions in a row turns into an interview, and interviews make people want to leave.
- **Go sideways off a detail.** They mention where they're from — react to something specific about it rather than moving to the next form field.
- **Offer an opinion.** Mild, playful disagreement generates far more conversation than agreement does, and shows there's a person in there.
- **Let silences sit for a beat.** Rushing to fill every pause reads as anxiety; a comfortable pause reads as ease.
- **Use callbacks.** Referring to something from ninety seconds ago creates the feeling of a shared history — the cheapest intimacy there is.

## The Reciprocity Test

Sixty seconds in, check whether the green light survived contact. This is the whole read, and it's simple:

- Are they **giving you material** — details you didn't ask for, tangents, questions back?
- Are they **matching your investment** — similar length answers, similar energy?
- Has their **body opened further** or closed?

Interest looks like effort. If they're doing work to keep it going, you're in. If you're carrying all of it — short answers, no questions returned, no volunteering — that's the soft no from [Offline Signals](nonverbal-signals.md), and the right move is a warm exit, not more effort.

## Common Stalls

- **One-word answers** — try one statement with an opinion in it. If that doesn't move it, it's a no, not a puzzle.
- **Too loud to talk** — move the conversation, not your voice: "it's impossible in here — come over by the bar for a second."
- **They're with friends** — greet the group, not just the person. Winning over the friends is often the entire task; ignoring them guarantees an extraction.
- **You go blank** — say so plainly. "I had something better than this planned" is charming; visible panic-scrambling isn't.
- **They seem nervous, not uninterested** — take the load off: talk a little more, ask less, give them easy things to respond to. Nerves and disinterest look similar for the first minute.

## Landing It

Don't let a good conversation dissolve. When it's going well — and preferably while it's still going well, not after it's flattened:

- End on a high rather than staying until the energy runs out
- Make the concrete ask: a specific plan with a day attached
- Get the contact details to actually use, then leave first
- Two or three minutes is a complete, successful interaction — it doesn't need to become an hour to count

And if you misread the cue entirely: "no worries, enjoy your night," and go. Exiting cleanly is what makes it costless to try again — see [Resilience and Handling Setbacks](resilience-setbacks.md).
